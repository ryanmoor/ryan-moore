# Research Paper — engagement brief

## The problem
As the new junior congressperson from Hawaii assigned to the House Judiciary Subcommittee on the Administrative State, Regulatory Reform, and Antitrust, you need to deliver policy goals to be included in the draft of a new bill that will be introduced on the House floor in the upcoming term. Your target is aggregators, internet-based companies that seem to exert extreme levels of market dominance in some cases (e.g. Google, Facebook), that current antitrust law doesn’t address very well. You need to choose a policy position that is a better fit, so you are looking at stricter merger and acquisition review as an option.

Your bill needs to have real substance without being too ambitious so it doesn’t die in committee, and it needs to not be watered down to the point of adding compliance cost without meaningfully changing outcomes. You need to ensure your definitions and reasoning are sound, because statutes are much harder to change than regulations written by administrative agencies. Lastly, you don’t want your bill to lead to over-regulation of businesses that your constituents enjoy–not only would this be unpopular, it could also destroy real value.

## What I am assuming
An aggregator is an internet-based company that has a direct relationship with users (consumers), near-zero marginal cost of serving those users, and falling user acquisition costs due to strong network effects. It is assumed that users have choice in whether to use an aggregator, but this is an assumption worth testing. Federal agencies will continue to have power to enforce laws at their current capacity.

With more time, it would be worth studying whether aggregators actually represent a market working properly over the long run. If consumers truly exercise a preference for aggregators as a superior experience, then perhaps market equilibrium equals a monopoly that has captured all suppliers and all consumers.

## Hypothesis
While aggregators may create a good experience for which consumers show a preference in the near term, they harm future consumers by stifling innovation and deterring future startups (suppliers) that would either compete with or complement incumbent platforms. From 2014 to 2019, the number of early-stage venture capital deals in social media fell by about 74%, from about 2,220 to about 580 (Figure 1a), and from 2016 to 2019, total funding for early-stage social media startups fell from about $3.07B to about $710M, representing about a 77% drop. This data is consistent with the idea that companies and products that would otherwise be brought to market are never created due to the risk of being commoditized and stripped of their value, or acquired and shut down. Therefore, your hypothesis is that new policy with stronger M&A review requirements will limit acquisitions of competitors and complements by deterring, or at least delaying deals, and will lead to a recovery of early-stage investing in the space.

## How I would know I was wrong
If the number of early-stage VC deals or early-stage VC investment recover without a preceding change in policy, then our assumption about the “kill zone” effect was incorrect, and the recent decline in both areas must have another cause. If a bill requiring stricter review is passed, and declining early-stage investment continues, then our assumption of its cause was incorrect. This would require the passage of a bill and subsequent years of data so it is outside the scope of this paper; however it would be worthy of further study.
