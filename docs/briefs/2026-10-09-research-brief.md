# Research Paper — engagement brief

## The problem
As a junior congressperson from Hawaii, newly assigned to the House Judiciary Subcommittee on the Administrative State, Regulatory Reform, and Antitrust, you need to deliver policy goals to be included in the draft of a new bill that will be introduced on the House floor later this term. Your target is aggregators, internet-based companies that seem to exert extreme levels of market dominance in some cases (e.g. Google, Facebook), that current legal standards don’t address very well. You need to develop a policy position that is a better fit, so you are considering a couple options–stricter merger and acquisition review, and restrictions on self-preferencing.

Your bill needs to have real substance without being too ambitious so it doesn’t die in committee, and it needs to not be watered down to the point of adding compliance cost without meaningfully changing outcomes. You need to ensure your definitions and reasoning are sound, because statutes are much harder to change than regulations written by administrative agencies. Lastly, you don’t want your bill to lead to over-regulation of businesses that your constituents enjoy–not only would this be unpopular, it could also destroy real value.

## What I am assuming
An aggregator is an internet-based company that has a direct relationship with users (consumers), near-zero marginal cost of serving those users, and falling user acquisition costs due to strong network effects. It is assumed that users have choice in whether to use an aggregator, but this is an assumption worth testing. Federal agencies will continue to have power to enforce laws at their current capacity.

With more time, it would be worth studying whether aggregators actually represent a market working properly. If consumer choice does exist and users are simply exercising their preference for a superior experience, then perhaps market equilibrium equals a monopoly that has captured all suppliers and all consumers.

## Hypothesis
While aggregators may create a good experience for which consumers show a preference in the near term, they harm future consumers by deterring future suppliers. Products that would otherwise be brought to market end up never being produced due to the risk of their being commoditized and stripped of their value. Therefore, the focus for regulators should be on limiting horizontal expansion of aggregators through stricter M&A review, and restrictions on self-preferencing of their own products (i.e. competing with their suppliers).

## How I would know I was wrong
If commoditization causes no harm to suppliers and they are able to realize sufficient economic value from using aggregators, then my hypothesis falls apart. If aggregators do not show anti-competitive behavior or market dominance with horizontal expansion, then legislation strengthening M&A review is unnecessary. If there is no evidence that aggregators attempt to compete with their suppliers by copying and/or preferencing their own products, then ex-ante restrictions run the risk of over-regulation, stifling innovation, and chilling an otherwise dynamic marketplace.
