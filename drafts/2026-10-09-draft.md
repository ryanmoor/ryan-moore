What is an Aggregator? (120 words)
The rise of the internet and digital markets has made way for certain companies to concentrate power in new ways. In contrast to platforms like Microsoft Windows, which enabled lots of new, profitable companies to be built on top of its technology, some platforms today exhibit different, problematic behavior. These new companies still facilitate exchange between sellers and buyers, create value by lowering search and transaction costs, and enable new suppliers to get started. But instead of enabling partner companies to thrive, they gobble up all the economic value for themselves in a winner-take-all or winner-take-most fashion, leaving little to no value for the suppliers who use the service. They solve what used to be every supplier’s biggest problem, distribution, by connecting them to a huge market and reducing transaction costs to effectively zero, but commoditize all products in doing so. They facilitate tons of economic activity, but force both suppliers and consumers to engage entirely on their terms.

Conclusion (30 words)
Company breakups, for example, have proven ineffective, because another aggregator can come along afterward to fill the gap.

Unlike traditional monopolies that control supply and distribution, aggregators derive power from controlling demand. Marginal cost of serving each additional user is effectively zero, and greater scale creates greater value. A virtuous cycle results from good user experience that attracts more users, which attracts more suppliers, which then creates a better user experience. This gives the appearance of consumer choice–alternatives are only “one click away”–however, it can be argued that aggregator behavior like commoditizing supplier content is anti-competitive and raises switching costs.

Aggregators present a real regulatory problem. Current and historical antitrust frameworks that focus on competition and consumer welfare were built to regulate markets for physical goods, and are inadequate for evaluating and correcting the harm to both suppliers and consumers in present-day digital markets.


Regulations that attempt to limit “vertical foreclosure” by suppliers–that is, the capturing of upstream and downstream value in order to limit competition–are insufficient, because aggregators accrue power by controlling demand more so than supply. Likewise, focusing on consumer welfare proves insufficient as well, because market dominance by aggregators rarely results in high prices. In fact, by some measures, aggregators create large consumer surplus rather than harm. (source)

Breaking these companies up is unlikely to work, because nothing prevents a new aggregator from stepping in to take its predecessor’s place.


Legislation will not address the basic principles that make aggregators work (near-zero marginal cost, strong network effects, user preference for the superior service, etc.) or any horizontal M&A deals that have already taken place (e.g. Facebook acquiring Instagram). What it can do is create levers that regulators can use to prevent market dominance occurring in the future.
