Aggregators and M&A Review: A New Policy Perspective
Ryan Moore


Introduction
Markets for consumer goods are made up of a value chain that includes suppliers, distributors and consumers. Any company that can gain a vast majority of market share in one of these three areas (horizontal monopoly), or that can integrate supply and distribution, can create market dominance and earn outsize profits. This is why, in the pre-internet era, distributors of various goods (newspapers, taxi companies, etc.) often integrated “backward” (up the value chain) to become suppliers as well (Thompson, 2015).
The internet era has created new markets for digital goods that exhibit a very different dynamic. Now that information is essentially free to everyone, it is no longer distribution that poses the biggest challenge to suppliers, but discovery. Distributors of digital goods are now able to integrate distribution forward (down the value chain rather than up) by “aggregating” both supply (content) and demand (users), thereby gaining tremendous market power on both sides (Thompson, 2015).

What is an Aggregator?
Aggregators are internet businesses that have a direct relationship with users (consumers), zero marginal cost of serving those users (adding more users cost nothing), and that capture significant network effects as they grow. Google and Facebook are the two most prominent examples of aggregators where we can see these network effects. As they gather more data, they become more valuable and attract more users. As the number of users grows, this attracts more suppliers, which attracts more users, and so on in a “virtuous cycle” (Thompson, 2017). The power these companies exert comes from, gathering all the consumers of a particular good (e.g. internet search, news content, hotel bookings) into one place. In doing so, they are able to curate and control which suppliers have market access, and capture a high percentage of the economic value of connecting suppliers and consumers.

Why Antitrust Falls Short
Current antitrust policy in the US focuses on consumer welfare, and has for about the last 35 years (Thompson, 2017). Companies that exert significant market power are often scrutinized for monopolistic practices such as charging high prices. The problem with applying this thinking to aggregators is that they don’t charge high prices. In fact, aggregators are generally free to use, are well-liked by users, and a strong case can even be made that they create significant consumer surplus (Zingales & Cowen, 2018). The fact that consumers choose to use them makes the consumer welfare standard a poor fit. To illustrate this point, consider internet advertising, which is dominated by two aggregators, Meta and Google. Their dominance has caused many to accuse them of having too much market power and to call for antitrust regulation. The problem with applying antitrust regulation here is that, if anything, they have improved advertising by lowering prices, opening up sellers to a much larger audience, and targeting users more effectively.

If consumer welfare is not the right policy approach to address the market power of aggregators, another option is to strengthen review requirements for mergers and acquisitions. Aggregators often use M&A to expand horizontally (e.g. Facebook acquiring Instagram) or to add new complementary features to their platform. Strengthening the review requirements for these deals is a way to prevent negative impacts like incumbents acquiring competitors simply to prevent them from competing, or expanding horizontally and gaining too much market power. Such a legislative approach may be a way to prevent long term harm to both suppliers and consumers.

Long-run Harm Despite Near-term Preference
In the short run, aggregators seem to have no downside for users. They instantly gain the ability to discover a large selection of products curated to their desires. But what about in the long run? Aggregators are not as friendly to suppliers in the short run. They force suppliers to participate entirely on their (the aggregator’s) terms, commoditize their content, and make it difficult for suppliers to profit. This may cause some to leave the platform, and discourages future suppliers from participating at all. 

Another, and probably the most consequential impact, is that aggregators discourage innovation and investment. One recent paper looked at venture capital funding for startups that compete with, or that seek to be complementary to incumbent platforms (specifically, Facebook and Google). They found that because incumbents acquire competitive and complementary startups quickly, venture capital investment in these startups has become unprofitable and has led to a steep decline in investment in the space (Kamepalli et al., 2022). This “kill zone” effect will reduce consumer welfare in the long run as innovation declines and eventually disappears. 

Recommendation:  Stricter M&A Review
Our recommendation is for stricter M&A review requirements in your policy agenda. From 2014 to 2019, the number of early-stage venture capital deals in social media fell by about 74%, from about 2,220 to about 580 (Figure 1a). From 2016 to 2019, total funding for early-stage social media startups fell from about $3.07B to about $710M, representing about a 77% drop. Both of these data examples are consistent with the “kill zone” effect that stricter review would address. A policy of stricter review would encourage greater innovation by deterring, or at least delaying acquisitions of both complements and competitors. It would also encourage greater investment in the space by returning VC funding to profitability. 

Conclusion and How to Know if This is Wrong
While consumers may continue to show a preference for aggregators in the near term, the data we observe that early-stage venture funding has declined significantly is consistent with the possibility of long-term decline in consumer welfare. Strengthening M&A requirements provides an alternative to applying current antitrust standards that will allow regulators to address the market dominance of aggregators and ensure long-term consumer benefit. However, if early-stage VC deals or investment recover without a preceding change in policy, then our assumption about the “kill zone” effect was incorrect, and the recent decline must have another cause.
