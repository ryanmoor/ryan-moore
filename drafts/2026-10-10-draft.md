Aggregators and M&A Review: A New Policy Perspective


Introduction (70 words)
Markets for consumer goods are made up of a value chain that includes suppliers, distributors and consumers. Any company that can gain a vast majority of market share in one of these three areas (horizontal monopoly), or that can integrate supply and distribution, can create market dominance and earn outsize profits. This is why, in the pre-internet era, distributors of various goods (newspapers, taxi companies, etc.) often integrated “backward” (up the value chain) to become suppliers as well (Thompson, 2015).

The internet era has created new markets for digital goods that exhibit a very different dynamic. Now that information is essentially free to everyone, it is no longer distribution that poses the biggest challenge to suppliers, but discovery. Instead of integrating backward with suppliers, distributors of digital goods have found an advantage in integrating forward with consumers. These internet-enabled companies are called aggregators (Thompson, 2015).

What is an Aggregator? (120 words)
Aggregators are internet businesses that have a direct relationship with users (consumers), zero marginal cost of serving those users (additional users cost nothing), and that capture significant network effects as they grow. Google and Facebook are the two most prominent examples of aggregators where we can see these network effects. As they gather more data, they become more valuable and attract more users. As the number of users grows, this attracts more suppliers, which attracts more users, and so on in a “virtuous cycle” (Thompson, 2017).

The power these companies exert comes not from aggregating supply (like the distributors of physical goods in the pre-internet era we mentioned before), but from aggregating demand–basically, gathering all the consumers of a particular good (e.g. internet search, news content, hotel bookings) into one place. In doing so, they are able to curate and control which suppliers have market access, and capture a high percentage of the economic value of connecting suppliers and consumers.

Why Antitrust Falls Short (120 words)
Current antitrust policy in the US focuses on consumer welfare, and has for about the last 35 years (Thompson, 2017). Companies that exert significant market power are often scrutinized for monopolistic practices and high prices, which are the primary way consumers might be harmed. The problem with applying this thinking to aggregators is that they don’t result in high prices. In fact, aggregators are generally free to use and are well liked by users, and a strong case can be even made that they create significant consumer surplus (Zingales & Cowen, 2018). The fact that consumers choose to use them makes the consumer welfare standard a poor fit. As an example, internet advertising is dominated by two aggregators, Meta and Google. Their dominance has caused many to accuse them of having too much market power and to call for antitrust regulation. The problem is that, if anything, they have made advertising much better by lowering prices and targeting users more effectively.

If consumer welfare is not the right policy approach to address the market power of aggregators, another option is to strengthen review requirements for mergers and acquisitions. So then, where is the harm if sellers get to connect with a large pool of prospective buyers, and buyers discover a much larger selection of products than they would be able to find elsewhere? The harm occurs in how the economic value of transactions is shared between sellers and the aggregator. Unlike dominant platforms in the past (e.g. Microsoft Windows) that took a modest percentage of the business facilitated by their technology, aggregators like Google and Facebook gobble up economic value in a winner-take-most fashion.

In the long run, this has potential to create harm to both sellers and buyers. In the short term, sellers are harmed in the way their content is commoditized. It’s very difficult for a producer to differentiate their products on an aggregator because everything is represented in exactly the same way.

Conclusion (30 words)
Company breakups, for example, have proven ineffective, because another aggregator can come along afterward to fill the gap.

Unlike traditional monopolies that control supply and distribution, aggregators derive power from controlling demand. Marginal cost of serving each additional user is effectively zero, and greater scale creates greater value. A virtuous cycle results from good user experience that attracts more users, which attracts more suppliers, which then creates a better user experience. This gives the appearance of consumer choice–alternatives are only “one click away”–however, it can be argued that aggregator behavior like commoditizing supplier content is anti-competitive and raises switching costs.


Breaking these companies up is unlikely to work, because nothing prevents a new aggregator from stepping in to take its predecessor’s place.

Legislation will not address the basic principles that make aggregators work (near-zero marginal cost, strong network effects, user preference for the superior service, etc.) or any horizontal M&A deals that have already taken place (e.g. Facebook acquiring Instagram). What it can do is create levers that regulators can use to prevent market dominance occurring in the future.
